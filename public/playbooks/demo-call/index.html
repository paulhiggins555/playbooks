Demo Call Playbook

A two call system for SaaS partners: discovery first, then a demo built only from what discovery found, so the prospect decides.

PHM DEMO CALL PLAYBOOK

Paul Higgins Mentoring | paulhigginsmentoring.com

Overview

This playbook turns a product tour that leaves prospects impressed and undecided into a two call system that produces a decision.

Discovery call first. Demo second, built only from what discovery found.

For SaaS partners eg: Salesforce, HubSpot, Zoho, Microsoft, Monday.com, NetSuite, Acumatica, Odoo who sell implementation, managed services or a productized solution into deals between $10K and $250K.

You are usually the founder plus one or two people. Your demos get compliments and stall.

The problem is almost never the product. It is that the demo is doing the discovery's job.

A demo is not where you find out what they need. It is where you prove you already know.

The Three Rules

Everything else in this playbook is detail. If you only change three things, change these.

1. No demo without a discovery call first. If the prospect refuses one, that is qualification data, not an inconvenience.

2. Three features. Never four. Every feature must tie to a sentence the prospect actually said. If you cannot quote them, cut it.

3. Announce the ask in the agenda. Say out loud, in the first two minutes, exactly what you will ask for at the end. Then ask for it.

Partners who do only these three usually get most of the result. The rest of this document is how.

Step-by-Step Process

1 - Make the Demo Easy to Book and Hard to Waste

Time: 2 hours setup

What you are doing: Putting the booking link everywhere, then putting a form in front of it that lets bad prospects remove themselves.

Why this works: Over half the partners reviewed in Dustin Purier's B2B SaaS peer review group hide their demo registration on a contact page or in the footer. At the same time, an unqualified demo costs you the same hour as a qualified one and returns nothing. Make it loud, then make it selective.

The form is the filter, not the call. If you charge per user and you do not want a two person shop, do not make "1 to 5 users" the first option in your dropdown. Start the range where your business actually starts. This is the single highest leverage change in the whole playbook.

How to execute:

  • Demo CTA in the top navigation, in the hero, and at the foot of every page. Same wording every time.
  • Build the form from Template 1. Four to six fields maximum.
  • Set your smallest dropdown option at or near your minimum viable deal.
  • Include one free text field: "What do you need to see to make a decision?" That answer builds your demo.
  • Build three reminders: confirmation on booking, 24 hours out, 1 hour out. The 24 hour email restates the agenda and the ask, so nobody is surprised at the close.
  • Route anyone below your floor to nurture, not to your calendar.

Common mistake: Treating form abandonment as a problem. A prospect who abandons because the smallest option was 50 users just saved you an hour. That is the form working.

Pro tip: Ask for additional stakeholders in the booking confirmation, right after they commit. That is the moment of highest compliance you will get.

2 - Run the Discovery Call

Time: 30 minutes

What you are doing: A separate 30 minute call whose only job is to decide whether a demo happens, what it shows, and who attends.

Why this works: Gong's analysis of over 500,000 discovery calls found sellers who won asked 15 to 16 questions, while sellers who lost asked around 20. A loser asks 20 because none of them landed.

The discovery call also fixes the stakeholder problem before it becomes one. Single threaded deals close at around 5 percent. Deals with five or more engaged stakeholders close at around 30 percent. You cannot multi thread a demo you have already booked with one person.

How to execute:

  • Open with the problem, not your company. "Tell me what made you book this."
  • Get one number out of them. No number means you have not found the pain.
  • Ask the two status quo cost questions from Template 2. They do more work than anything else on the call.
  • Map the room: who is affected, who signs, who could kill it.
  • Name your three features out loud and confirm: "So on the demo I'll show you A, B and C. Anything else you need to see?"
  • Book the demo before the call ends, with names attached.

Common mistake: Using discovery to explain your methodology. Diagnosis is free. Prescription is paid. The moment you explain how you would fix it, you have done the consulting for nothing.

Pro tip: If you cannot fill the demo scope from the transcript, do not book the demo. Send one email with the missing questions. A prospect who will not answer three questions by email will not sign a $40K contract.

3 - Build the Demo From Discovery, Not From the Product

Time: 30 minutes

What you are doing: Three features, one deck, a clean environment.

Why this works: Gong's analysis of over 3 million product demos found top performing reps spend 39 percent less time talking about features than average reps, and get 28 percent more questions from buyers. The reps who show less get asked more. That is the whole game.

The deck drives the demo, not the product. The moment you open with a screen share you are showing an interface to someone with no frame for it. Everything looks like noise. The deck controls pacing and stops you wandering.

If you sell on a large platform, the temptation is to prove breadth. Breadth is what makes you interchangeable with every other partner on that platform.

How to execute:

  • Write the prospect's exact sentence next to each of your three features. No sentence, no feature.
  • Adapt the deck from Template 3. One deck per vertical, never rebuilt per prospect.
  • Load the demo environment with their industry, their terminology, plausible volumes. Odoo's own partner guidance tells partners to use the customer's products and logo.
  • Show the outcome first, then the steps that produced it.
  • Time box each feature to 4 to 6 minutes. Three features is 15 to 18 minutes of product. The rest is conversation.

Common mistake: The harbour tour. Showing everything, hoping something lands. They do not want to see everything your platform does. They want to see the thing that ends the problem they described.

Pro tip: If you have made the WHO decision properly, three or four decks cover your entire pipeline and prep drops to ten minutes.

4 - Run the Demo

Time: 45 minutes

What you are doing: Announcing the ask, spending the first fifth of the call still on slide two, then challenging rather than explaining.

Why this works: Most presenters drop the agenda slide because it feels like filler. It is the opposite. The agenda is where you say out loud that you will ask for something specific at the end. That removes the ambush and gives the call a destination.

Spending 20 percent of the demo on the intro slide feels wrong and is right. If you have not got engagement on slide one, they are on their phone by slide three.

On the running of it, here is the honest data. Gong found the talk to listen ratio on winning demo calls was 65:35 and on losing demo calls 66:34. No meaningful difference. Talk ratio is a discovery lever, not a demo lever. What separates winners is that buyers ask 28 percent more questions in top performers' demos, and top performers keep any single monologue under about 106 seconds. The mechanic is not "talk less." It is "make them ask."

One split matters more than any other. Decision makers and influencers are watching different films. The influencer pictures themselves using the thing to solve a problem. The decision maker pictures themselves no longer being bothered by the influencer who cannot get it solved. Same slide, two jobs. Say both lines out loud.

How to execute:

  • Slide 1: who is on the call and why. Slide 2: the agenda, with the ask stated plainly (Template 4).
  • Stay on slide 2. Confirm the problem back and ask them to correct you.
  • Do not advance until someone other than your original contact has spoken at length.
  • Per feature: state what it ends, show the outcome, restate it against their number from discovery.
  • Ask a challenge question on every feature. Not "does that make sense" but "how are you handling that today, and what does it cost when it breaks?"
  • If you have been talking 90 seconds, ask something.
  • If a stakeholder has not spoken in ten minutes, ask them an easy question by name.
  • On price: give a range tied to scope, then return to their number. "You said the current process costs about $90K a year. This sits between $30K and $45K depending on scope."
  • On competitors: never claim you have none. Name doing nothing, going direct, and one real alternative, and say where each is genuinely the better choice.
  • Log every objection verbatim. Any objection raised twice becomes an appendix slide before your next demo.

Common mistake: Explaining instead of challenging. Weak demos are explanations delivered at a prospect. Strong demos are a series of moments where you ask them to prove the problem is real. The goal is a fast no as much as a yes. A bad customer is the most expensive customer you will ever onboard.

Pro tip: Problem story, not problem statement. A statement is "manufacturers lose margin on manual quoting." A story is "the last three quoting projects I ran started the same way: the owner was the only one who knew the pricing, and every quote waited for him." A statement is information. A story is recognition.

Second pro tip: If the room goes quiet, stay quiet. People listen for gaps in conversation to know when to enter. Let it get uncomfortable. They will look up.

5 - Close and Follow Up the Same Day

Time: 20 minutes after the call

What you are doing: Landing the ask you announced, filling the mutual action plan in live, sending the follow up within four hours.

Why this works: Post demo is the graveyard. Almost every partner in Dustin Purier's peer review group, including him, admitted it. The call goes well, everyone is enthusiastic, then nothing happens because nobody named the next action while the enthusiasm was in the room.

Mutual action plans are associated with roughly 26 percent higher win rates (Outreach, vendor research, treat as directional). The mechanism is not directional: a written next step with a name and a date beats "we'll be in touch" every time.

How to execute:

  • Land the ask exactly as you framed it in the agenda. Say it once. Then stop talking.
  • Yes: put the next date in the calendar while everyone is still on the call.
  • No: ask "what would have had to be different?" That answer is worth more than the deal.
  • "We need to discuss internally": ask who is in that discussion and offer to send a one page summary for it. That gets you into the room you were not invited to.
  • Fill the mutual action plan (Template 6) on screen in the last three minutes, with them. Co authored is theirs. Emailed afterwards is yours, and yours gets ignored.
  • Send the follow up (Template 5) within four hours. Never next morning.
  • Anyone not closed within 14 days goes to nurture. Not because they forgot your product. Because they got busy.

Common mistake: A generic recap. If the email could be sent to any prospect with the name swapped, it is a template, not a follow up. Quote two things they actually said.

Pro tip: Nurture cadence that works: week 2 a relevant client outcome with no ask, week 4 the most common objection answered properly, week 6 what doing nothing actually costs, week 8 "has this moved up or down the list since we spoke?", week 12 clean exit.

When they do it out loud, they sell themselves. Question 14 finds the person who kills the deal in week six, before you have invested six weeks.

Why this works: Slides 9 and 10 are the influencer and the decision maker. Naming them as separate slides forces you to speak to both rather than assuming one translates for the other. Slide 13 repeating slide 2 word for word means the ask lands as a scheduled item, not an ambush.

If it stalls, the fix depends on where:

Where it stalls

What to fix

They do not book or do not show

The offer and the form. Messaging, not sales.

Good call, then silence

The ask. You did not announce it, or you announced it softly.

Enthusiasm, then drift

The mutual action plan. Nobody named a date while the room was warm.

The qualifying form. Your ranges start too low.

If you are not at 30 percent demo to close by week eight, the problem is almost never the demo. Go back to Step 2. A demo that does not convert is usually a discovery call that did not produce a number.

Next Steps

Today, 15 minutes: Open your website. Count the pages with a visible demo booking link above the fold. If it is fewer than all of them, that is your first job.

This week, 2 hours: Build the form from Template 1 with ranges set at your real floor. Build the three reminders. Turn the automation on.

End of week 2: Build the deck from Template 3. Version one only. Ten slides is enough.

End of month 1: Five demos under this system. Every one preceded by discovery. Every one three features. Every one followed up same day. Log every objection.

End of month 3: Objection slides built for your top three. Demo to close measured over a real sample.

The demo is not where you find out whether they need you. It is where you prove you already understood. If your demo is doing discovery's job, you are not losing on product. You are losing on preparation.

Evidence Notes

Before you quote any number here to a client.

Gong is the only large sample source in this playbook (over 500,000 discovery calls, over 3 million demos). The question count, the 28 percent more buyer questions, the 39 percent less feature talk and the 106 second monologue ceiling all come from that work and are solid.

Worth knowing: Gong found the talk to listen ratio on winning demos was 65:35 against 66:34 on losing demos. No meaningful difference. Anyone selling you "talk less on demos" is quoting the wrong study.

Everything else quoted here is vendor sourced with no sample size disclosed: the 73 percent conversion drop without discovery, the 20 to 40 percent no show baseline, the 26 percent mutual action plan lift, the single versus multi threaded close rates. Every one comes from a company selling something the number supports. The direction is consistent across sources. The precision is not defensible. Use the direction, not the decimal.

No reliable data exists on optimal demo length, the ideal number of features shown, or whether price belongs mid demo or at the end. Where this playbook takes a position on those, it is practitioner pattern, not measurement.
