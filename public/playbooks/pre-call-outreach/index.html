THE DOCUMENT SET

This playbook is the method. One project holds the tooling and three companions sit alongside it. The Business Coach project in your AI assistant is the central home where every outreach chat runs, every skill is installed, and the client profile lives. The Message Library Playbook holds every message and the rules behind them. The Sales Navigator Search Playbook builds the searches. The Skills Library Playbook holds the seven skills and how they chain. Two earlier playbooks come before this one: Niche Selection and Validation and Big Promise Development.

The Business Coach Project

The Business Coach project is one project in your AI assistant that all outreach chats run inside. Not one project per topic, not one chat per skill. One project, many chats. Every prospect becomes a chat inside that project, named after them so it can be found by search later.

The project loads three things on every chat: the project instructions (who you are, what you sell, how you talk), the seven skills, and the pointers to your client profile files. That means a fresh chat about a new prospect already knows your ICP, your voice rules, your message rules and your language bank. You do not paste any of it in.

The alternative — running standalone chats outside a project — is what most people default to, and it is the reason their assistant answers each question from scratch and forgets what they told it last week. A project fixes both. One home, one context, every skill available.

The rule. One prospect, one chat, inside the Business Coach project. Name the chat after the prospect. That is how you find them again a week later, and how the project stays clean enough to work in.

Overview

This playbook takes you from scattered LinkedIn activity to a repeatable daily motion that produces qualified calls. It covers the pre-call half of the sales process only. Once a call is booked and the intake answers are in, it hands over to the call process.

This is for SaaS partners (Salesforce, Zoho, Microsoft, Monday, HubSpot, NetSuite, Acumatica, Odoo or similar) who want to build consistent revenue outside their vendor.

Two things sit alongside it. Your Client Profile holds everything specific to you, and it is yours. Seven skills do the repetitive work, and they are written out generically in the Skills Library Playbook so you can copy them into your own assistant. This playbook is generic and gets updated centrally, so nothing with your name, a number or a price in it belongs here.

Same buyer, same question, a minimum of one hour a day. Do that and the pipeline stops being a mystery and becomes a number you can move.

The Workflow Reversal

The single biggest change in v1.1 is who writes the message. In v1.0 the Outreach skill drafted, you edited. That workflow produced drafts that sounded like AI trying to sound like you, and it hid the fact that most of the useful thinking was happening at the edit stage anyway.

v1.1 reverses it. You write every draft yourself, using the format spines in the Message Library Playbook as the starting shape. Then the Outreach Critique skill reads the draft against your rules, your language bank and your prior corrections, and returns either SEND or a numbered list of what breaks, worst first, with the source named for each flag and a rewrite offered.

Two things happen when you flip it this way. The messages sound like you from message one, because they are yours. And every edit you make is a data point the Messages Learning skill logs back into your rules, so the critique gets sharper every week instead of the drafts staying equally generic forever.

You write. The skill critiques. Never the other way round.

Pre-Flight Checklist

Complete every item before you send a connection request to a stranger. Tier one sources are the exception: you can start those today, which is the point of the ordering.

  • One vertical chosen and validated through the Niche Selection and Validation Multiplier. If you have not done that, stop and do it first. Outreach without a vertical is the most expensive mistake in this document.
  • Your ICP written down, including the anti-client, the hard disqualifiers and the ranked triggers, not just who you sell to.
  • Their language captured from three to five recorded calls, with the surface symptom separated from the core problem. Stored in your Client Language Bank.
  • Source audit run across all fourteen sources, so you know how many names you already have before you buy any.
  • LinkedIn profile rewritten customer focused, not platform focused. Headline, banner, About, Featured, Experience. The Featured section carries one asset that answers what you do and who for, in under two minutes.
  • Booking link live and tested, with the intake questions attached and mandatory.
  • Website matches the profile. A prospect who checks both and finds two different businesses stops replying.
  • Proof bank filled. Three named client outcomes with a number attached to each. Kept in your Message Rules so the critique skill can enforce it.
  • Sales Navigator saved searches returning 750 to 1,000 results in total. Built with the Sales Navigator Search Playbook. Needed for tier five only, so it does not block your start.
  • Tracking in place in your CRM with the touch number, the source and the next action date for every contact.
  • The Business Coach project set up in your AI assistant, with the seven skills installed and pointed at your client profile files in your shared Drive.
  • Weekly LinkedIn posts about you and the results you get clients, so that when prospects check your profile before replying they already know both.

Hard stop. Do not send to strangers until the profile is rewritten, the Featured asset is live, and the proof bank has three confirmed entries. Every message drives traffic to your profile. If the profile is not ready you are spending your best contacts to prove you are not ready.

The Six Steps

Steps 1 and 2 are gates, not projects. Choosing a vertical and defining the ideal client inside it is its own piece of work with its own playbook: Niche Selection and Validation Multiplier. If you have not done it, stop here and do that first. What these two steps do is confirm the output is specific enough to build a list from, and add the two things outreach needs that niche selection does not produce, which are the anti-client and the ranked triggers.

Step 1: Pick the Vertical

What you are doing. Choosing one market. Not a customer, a market. A group of companies that share an industry, a size, a platform and a geography, so that what you learn from one of them is worth something with the next.

Why this works. A vertical compounds and a general market doesn't. Your tenth conversation inside one vertical beats your first, because you're carrying nine other people's language into it: what breaks, what it costs, what they've tried, what they call it. None of that transfers if every conversation is in a different industry.

The specificity test

Your vertical passes if you can fill five variables in one sentence: industry, company size, revenue band, platform or technology, region. If you cannot fill all five, you have a category rather than a vertical.

Not a vertical

A vertical

Manufacturing

Food processing companies, 50 to 200 employees, running [platform], in [region]

Professional services

Estate planning law firms, one to five attorneys, 300K to 1.5M revenue, United States

Ecommerce

Consumer goods brands selling into retail, 20 to 100 staff, on [platform], Australia and New Zealand

The test that settles arguments. Read your vertical out loud to someone who does not work with you and ask them to name three companies that fit. If they can, it is a vertical. If they ask a clarifying question first, it is not specific enough yet.

One vertical at a time. Two verticals means two message libraries, two source audits and two sets of language to keep straight, and the compounding that makes a vertical worth choosing never starts in either of them.
What picking a vertical does not mean

This is a marketing decision, not a revenue policy. The vertical decides where your outbound hours go, what your profile says, what you post about and who you research. It does not decide what work you are allowed to accept.

Referral work from outside the vertical is still good revenue. Take it. Turning down a well-paid referral to stay pure is a cost with no return, and it's usually why people refuse to narrow in the first place. What you don't do is start marketing to that adjacent industry because one good client came from it. That's how a vertical quietly becomes a category again.

The line to hold. Inbound and referral: take the work if it is good work. Outbound, content and profile: one vertical only. If you find yourself writing a second set of messages for referral work, the vertical has slipped and it is worth stopping to notice.

Step 2: Define the Ideal Client

What you are doing. Narrowing from the market to the buyer. Inside the vertical, which companies specifically, which person inside them signs, which ones you decline, and which public signals mean now rather than someday.

Why this works. The vertical tells you where to look. The ideal client tells you who to write to: the message is written to a person, not a market. Deep personalisation lifts reply rates by up to 112 per cent against generic outreach, and you can't personalise deeply against an industry.

Get their exact language before you write anything

This is the part that separates outreach that gets replies from outreach that gets ignored, and it takes an afternoon. You're not inventing how your buyer describes their problem, you're collecting it.

  • Pull three to five recorded calls where a prospect or client explained their situation in their own words. Sales calls, discovery calls, kickoff calls, anything where they were talking and you were listening. If nothing is recorded, book two research interviews instead and record those. If neither is possible, reconstruct from memory, but recorded is worth ten times reconstructed.
  • Extract three things from each: the exact words and phrases, the metaphors and comparisons, and the tone: frustrated, resigned, embarrassed and hopeful all read differently.
  • Note what they say first versus what comes out twenty minutes in. The first thing is the surface symptom. The later thing is usually closer to the real problem.
  • Look for the phrases that repeat across different people. Those are the ones that go straight into your messages, unedited.

Store the output in your Client Language Bank, which is one of the five files the skills read. The Outreach Critique and Prospect Prep skills both use it.

Why this matters more than any wording advice. Every template in this set has a bracket in it. The brackets are where their language goes. Fill them with your language and the message reads like a consultant. Fill them with theirs and it reads like someone who has had this conversation before.
Surface symptom versus core problem, and which one you write about

What a buyer will admit to isn't the same as what's actually wrong. Take the stated complaint and ask why five times until you hit something structural. 'We can't find enough clients' runs down through competing with forty others, to generic positioning, to being afraid to turn work away, to not believing they can charge a premium with a narrow focus. The complaint is the symptom. The last one is the problem.

Both matter, and they do different jobs.

The most common outreach mistake sits here. People write their outreach about the core problem, because that is the impressive insight and the thing they solve. It does not work. The buyer reads a message about a problem they have not conceded and moves on. Lead with the symptom they will admit to, earn the conversation, then name the problem on the call.

The full method for excavating the core problem and turning it into positioning is its own playbook: Big Promise Development Multiplier. What this step needs from it is two things: the language file, and a clear split between the symptom you write about and the problem you sell against.

How to execute
  • Write the company profile inside the vertical: size, revenue band, structure, how they operate today, and what they are currently using instead of you.
  • Name the person. Exact titles, decision makers only. If two people are involved, say which one you write to and which one they have to convince.
  • Build the language file from three to five recorded calls, using the method above. Store it in your Client Language Bank.
  • Write the surface symptom in their words, and the core problem in yours. Keep them clearly separated, because they are used in different places.
  • Write the anti-client. The profiles you will decline, and the profiles you will only take at a premium. Write the reason next to each. This is the half most people skip and it is the half that makes the definition real.
  • Write four or five hard disqualifiers that mean skip with no judgement call required.
  • Rank your triggers. A trigger is a public event that means the problem you solve just got more expensive for them. Rank by strength, because your best hours go to the strongest.
  • Store all of it in your ICP, not in this playbook and not in your head. Client Selection and Outreach Critique both read it on every name.

Step 3: Build the Target List

What you are doing. Turning the definition into named people, each with enough context attached that the first message can be about them rather than about you.

Why this works. This step decides everything downstream and is the one almost everyone gets wrong, because they open Sales Navigator on day one and start with total strangers. There are fourteen places to find your buyer and Sales Navigator is thirteenth. Cold platform-wide acceptance runs around 28 per cent. Retargeting people who engaged with your content runs 72 per cent. Same effort, more than double the result, and the warm sources cost nothing.

Groups deserve a second look. They have a poor reputation and most people skip them, which is exactly why they work. Being in the same group lets you message someone directly without a connection request, so the acceptance step disappears entirely.
Volume

Volume is gated by source, not by the calendar. Work tiers one to three at whatever rate they yield. Only once those are dry do you move to tier four and five at 50 a week, and only once that is holding do you go to 100. One hundred a week is the ceiling and it stays the ceiling. Practitioners running this at scale cap at 30 messages a day per person to avoid throttling, so 100 a week sits safely underneath.

How to execute
  • Run the source audit before you build anything. One hour. It usually turns up several hundred names you already had access to.
  • Set the source mix for the week and record it, so you can see which source actually produces calls.
  • Score each name. Run Client Selection in your Business Coach project. It returns IN or OUT with a one-line reason. A maybe is an OUT, recorded with a reason. Read those reasons monthly: they're how the ICP sharpens.
  • Build the prospect file for every IN. Run Prospect Prep. The skill fills every field except the observation line.
  • Write the observation line yourself. One sentence on what genuinely stood out, not what you think will land. This is the only field the skill can't do for you, and the field the reader responds to.

Common mistakes. Starting at source thirteen. Exporting a list of 500 and treating that as the work. Letting the skill write the observation line. Building files for people you have not scored.

Pro tip. Track which source each booked call came from. Within two months one or two sources will be producing most of them. Everyone assumes it will be Sales Navigator. It usually is not.

Step 4: Four Touch Outreach

What you are doing. Opening a conversation with one question and asking it four different ways over fourteen days. Not four different questions: one question, four angles.

Why this works. A thread that runs on one question reads like a person who is curious. A thread that asks something new each time reads like a sequence, because it is one. Around 80 per cent of replies arrive after a follow-up, and returns collapse after the fourth touch, from 2.9 per cent down to 1.5. So four touches, then stop.

Why the connection request is always blank

Across every recent dataset, no-note invitations beat noted ones on acceptance. One study of more than 80,000 requests puts blank at 55 to 68 per cent against 28 to 45 for noted. A 47-campaign study puts it at 38 against 29. A controlled 200-request test puts it at 34 against 18. Even a hyper-personalised note tops out around 60, below what a blank request achieves at its best.

So the note never earns its place. Everything you'd have written goes into T1 instead, where you've already been accepted and the message can be three times longer.

T4 fires on a reply, not on a schedule. The ask is built from something they said. If they have not said anything there is nothing to build it from, and sending it anyway turns a personal thread into a template. A silent thread gets the exit message instead.
Why the voice note is the strongest touch you have

A study of 100 LinkedIn voice notes recorded a 63 per cent response rate with under 3 per cent negative responses, and evening sends outperformed mornings and afternoons. The two-day text nudge afterwards recorded around 40 per cent on its own. One sales team that replaced InMails with voice notes recorded a sevenfold increase in replies. Nothing else in this sequence performs like that, which is why it sits at touch three rather than being treated as optional.

If a voice note is genuinely not possible for a given prospect (audio blocked, format mismatch), replace T3 with a written pattern break: a third angle on the same question, two or three lines, no repeat of prior content. The nudge at T3b still fires.

How to execute
  • You write every draft yourself, using the format spines in the Message Library Playbook as the starting shape. Not the skill.
  • Run Outreach Critique on the draft. The skill returns SEND or numbered flags worst first, each naming its source (a rule, a prior correction, a language bank entry). A rewrite is offered where relevant.
  • Send the connection request blank. Every tier, every time, no exceptions.
  • Send T1 between two and 48 hours after the accept. Not instantly, which reads as automation, and not after two days, when they have forgotten accepting. Context first, then the question.
  • If you are already connected, which is most of tiers one to three, skip the connection request and start at T1 with the context you already have.
  • Space the follow-ups at days five, ten and twelve. Never repeat prior content. Treat everything already sent as read, even though it probably was not.
  • Record the voice note in their evening, under 60 seconds, no pitch anywhere in it.
  • Fire T4 the moment they reply, at whatever point that happens. Quote their own words back.
  • Run Messages Learning on every send where the draft was edited between critique and dispatch. The block goes into the Messages log, and the REASON becomes a standing rule the critique skill applies next time.

Common mistakes. Putting a note on the connection request. Sending T4 into silence. Asking a different question at each touch. A calendar link before the third message. Opening with "quick question", which recorded a 1.0 per cent reply rate across 5,000 sends. Letting the skill draft the message.

Pro tip. Get one thread to a reply and read all four messages back end to end. If they do not read as one conversation, fix that before sending a hundred more.

Step 5: Handle the Reply

What you are doing. Turning a reply into either a named problem in their words, or a clean disqualification. Nothing else counts as progress.

Why this works. The reply is where most outreach dies, because the reflex is to pitch the moment someone engages. Cold pitches convert under 10 per cent. One or two exchanges before the ask converts far better, because the ask can then be built from what they actually said.

How to execute
  • Reply the same day. Speed is the whole advantage a small business has here.
  • Write the draft yourself. Run Outreach Critique on it. Every reply, every time.
  • Ask the question that surfaces cost. Not what's broken. What it costs when it breaks, in money, time or risk. Your version lives in your Qualifying Question.
  • Offer the call only once a problem is named, and use their words when you do.
  • Log the correction through Messages Learning before you close the session.

The rescuer trap. When they ask how you would fix it, the answer is not the fix. It is: that is what the call is for, right now I am working out whether I can actually help. Give the diagnosis away and you have nothing left to book a call about.

Common mistakes. Sending a booking link on first contact. Chasing more than twice after the ask. Treating a polite reply as interest, so the thread stalls in pleasantries. Presuming their internal state, which stops sounding real the second time you do it.

Pro tip. Keep a running list of the exact phrases people use to describe the problem. That list becomes your next round of message wording, your next post, and eventually your website copy.

Step 6: Book and Qualify

What you are doing. Putting a form in front of the calendar so the call is qualified before it is held and you walk in already knowing the answers.

Why this works. An unqualified 30-minute call costs an hour once prep and follow-up are counted. The form does the qualifying so the call can do the selling. It also filters: a buyer who won't spend three minutes on a form won't spend money either.

How to execute
  • Attach the intake questions to the booking link so a slot cannot be held without them.
  • Read the answers before the call, not during it. Re-asking what they already answered wastes your credibility and their time.
  • Apply the three way gate the same day the form arrives.
  • On a hold, set the revisit date immediately. A hold with no date is a loss with extra steps.
  • On a stop, say so plainly, send one useful thing, close the record and write the reason. The reasons are how the ICP improves.

Pro tip. Every question past ten costs you completions. Before you add one, remove one.

Getting the data into your CRM

The old answer was a browser extension that pushes a LinkedIn profile into the CRM in one click. Each CRM has its own, they cost money, and they all share the same flaw: what lands in the CRM is a raw contact: name, title, company, maybe an email. Everything that makes the record useful (the score, the trigger, the source, the observation line) still has to be added by hand afterwards.

The better answer is to let your assistant write the record, because by the time you want it in the CRM it already has all of it. Client Selection has scored the name. Prospect Prep has built the file. You have written the observation line. Writing that to the CRM is one more instruction in the same session, not a separate tool and a separate step.

Do not report inputs as headline numbers. Connections sent and messages written are effort. Replies and held calls are results. Reporting effort as achievement is how a system stays busy and unproductive for a quarter.

The circuit breaker. If held calls fall below your floor for a full month, stop building. No new tools, no new templates, no new systems. Outreach only, every morning, until it recovers. The instinct when the pipeline thins is to go and build something. That instinct is the problem.

Weekly, 15 minutes, same slot

  • Update the six numbers. Actual figures, not impressions
  • Read the symptom table above and identify the single weakest step
  • Change one thing. Only one, or you will not know what worked
  • Review the Messages log. After every ten entries, ask which two or three patterns keep showing up, and fix them at the rule level rather than one message at a time
  • Refill from the next source so Monday does not start empty
  • Anyone silent for 14 days after the exit message moves out of the active list and into the network

What counts as a signal. A direct reply, a booking, or a question about what you sell. Nothing else. A profile view is not a signal and a newsletter open is not a signal. Contacts with no signal in 90 days leave the active list. They stay in your network, they just stop consuming your mornings.

The first month

  • Today, one hour. Set up the Business Coach project in your AI assistant, install the seven skills, point them at the client profile files. Run the source audit. Filter your first connections against the ICP and send ten blank connection requests. No Sales Navigator needed, no saved search needed. In the market on day one
  • This week. Finish the pre-flight checklist. Write your ICP. Fill the proof bank with three confirmed client outcomes. First T1 messages out. First Messages Learning entries logged
  • Weeks two to four. Work tiers one to three until they are dry. Record acceptance and reply rates from the first week, not the fourth
  • Month two. Tier four and five at 50 a week, and only if acceptance is holding at 30 per cent or better. If it is not, the targeting is wrong and volume will not fix it
  • Month three. Up to 100 a week, the ceiling. Drafts need almost no editing because Messages Learning has fed corrections back into the critique skill. The weekly review is a habit rather than an event

Do not perfect it. Execute it. Your first 25 messages will not be good. You will learn more from 25 imperfect sends than from another week of refining the wording, because the edits you make after a real reply are worth more than the ones you make in a document — and every one of those edits goes back through Messages Learning into the rules.

Authority Content

Weekly

In: a market signal your buyer is discussing. Out: a post with your angle, plus a comment for the source

If you simply agree with the signal, there is no post. The angle has to be one only you would take

Content Comment

Daily, and before a first message

In: a post from your watchlist or from a scored prospect. Out: three comment options, each a different angle

Zero self-promotion. The comment earns the profile visit and the profile does the selling

The join between the two loops. A scored prospect who posted in the last seven days gets a comment before a connection request, not instead of one. They see your name once in a context where you were useful, then the request arrives two days later. That sequence costs nothing and it is the cheapest lift available to your acceptance rate.

Why the log is the whole thing. Over 30 to 50 logged corrections the first draft stops needing edits. Without the log the skills stay exactly as good as they were on day one, forever. One correction a week is the minimum. If you are editing nothing at all, either the skill has become very good or you are sending without reading properly.
