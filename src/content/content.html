How to use this playbook

This is a strategy document for any owner-led firm that sells expertise and uses content to fill its pipeline. It sets what you say, who you say it to, and the gate every piece passes before it goes out.

Keep channel SOPs separate. This document changes when strategy changes, not when execution details change. Where it conflicts with your live LinkedIn profile or website, the live positioning wins.

Fill in the bracketed fields once, then run every draft against it.

Audience

Write for one reader: the owner of a business already past a revenue floor [your floor, e.g. $500K], founder-led, who wants to grow revenue and build a team to deliver it. Revenue is coming in, but growth is flat, inconsistent, or dependent on referrals and one lead source.

Recognition rule: nobody calls themselves by your category label. They identify by the platform, industry or tool they work in. Name at least one of those early, ideally in the first line. Keep a fixed list of the names your readers use and never swap in vague labels like "business owners" or "consultants".

Who you are only matters when it does work. State your credentials plainly when the reader needs a reason to trust the point: years in the field, what you built or sold, how many people you have worked with. Never assume the reader knows them.

The one message

Pick one commercial message and hold it across every channel. A strong default: charge more for what you deliver and win new clients at higher rates, because that is what moves operating profit.

Anchor it in a real reason why now. Name the market shift in measured terms, for example AI absorbing some delivery hours or a supplier thinning your margin. Never overstate it. "Some of the work, not all of it, not overnight" keeps you credible.

Frame every shift as an opportunity, not a threat. A pricing change, a new AI release, a licence change: each is something your reader can now sell. The owner who sees it first and prices it gets paid. The owner who waits watches their work become a line item the client wants to cut.

Give the reader reassurance. They keep what already works and add on top. They do not need to reinvent the business.

Decision themes and rotation

Every core piece teaches one decision your method is built on. Most growth frameworks come down to three, in order:

  1. Who you serve. One focused market earns specialist rates. Test it for 90 days with a clear stop-or-commit point.
  2. What you sell. One offer on top of your existing work, priced as a product, fixed fee or retainer, never by the hour.
  3. How you fill the pipeline. Build capacity first, then own your lead sources (inbound, outbound, partners) so raising rates stops feeling risky.

Tag every draft internally with its theme before review, for example "WHAT: retainer offer". The tag never appears in the post. The theme decides what the post recommends.

Rotate so each decision gets airtime: week 1 leans Who, week 2 What, week 3 How, week 4 on the big-picture close. Market-signal posts take whichever theme the signal fits.

In short-form (posts, Shorts, carousels), show the decision in plain words and keep method labels out. In long-form (newsletter, podcast, YouTube, playbooks), name and teach the decisions. Use the full method name on your profile and on calls.

The altitude standard

Write for owners, not operators. Your reader makes decisions about money, risk and people, not settings, sends and setup. Written well, the same post is worth reading for the chair of a $50M business.

Every draft passes two tests:

  • The owner test. Is the subject a decision the owner makes, or a task someone else does? Tasks go back.
  • The boardroom test. Would the chair of a $50M business recognise a commercial decision they also face? If it only makes sense to someone who logs into the tool, it goes back.

Use the language of altitude: operating profit, margin, pricing power, revenue per head, concentration risk, cost of waiting, sellability. Use less of: workflows, tools, templates, hacks.

Strategic topic bank: pricing power, supplier risk, concentration risk, who owns the AI decision, capacity per head, founder dependence and sellability, the cost of waiting, specialist margins, owner time as capital, market signals read at P&L level.

Keep LinkedIn and the newsletter strategic. Tactics appear only as one proof line inside a strategic point. Tactical depth lives on the podcast, in playbooks and on calls.

The originality check

Your edge over generic AI content is recorded material: sales calls, client calls, peer calls and podcast episodes. A market-signal post that repeats what every vendor blog already said wastes the slot.

Run this check on every market-signal post, after the owner and boardroom tests and before the edit rules. Client and personal posts skip it, because a recorded result already makes them original.

  1. Pin down the topic, the angle and the proof. Mark each proof item as recorded (from a transcript) or recalled (from memory).
  2. Search the last 12 months: LinkedIn, industry newsletters and blogs, YouTube and podcast titles, and a watchlist of the five or six voices your readers follow.
  3. Pull the 3 to 5 closest matches and paraphrase them.
  4. Give one verdict: OPEN (nobody is saying this, or not from this angle), CROWDED (common topic, but your angle or proof differs) or SATURATED (topic and angle widely covered).
  5. Name where you are different, where you sound the same, and one call: publish, sharpen or drop.

OPEN moves on. CROWDED is sharpened: lead with your recorded proof and cut every phrase that sounds like the matches. SATURATED is dropped, or returns only with a recorded moment that gives a different angle.

Never invent proof, results or quotes to look distinct. Thin search results mean unclear, never OPEN by default.

Post structure and quality gate

Every post runs four moves, in order:

  1. The shift. A specific, checkable market fact or client number. Name the platform or industry in line one or two.
  2. The better read. The obvious reaction, then the opportunity in it. For client posts: the result, then the part actually worth copying.
  3. The move. What to sell and how to price it, or what the client did next, with a number and a date.
  4. The landing. The usual move against the better move, closed with a contrast pair.

Quality gate

Run it in order. Fail a step and the draft goes back:

  1. Owner test and boardroom test.
  2. Originality check (market-signal posts only).
  3. The reviewer can point to the theme, the opportunity, the move and the contrast pair.
  4. Edit rules.
  5. Voice standard. Then it goes to the owner for final edits.

Edit rules

Every draft passes these before it reaches the owner. They are the main quality gate.

  1. Lead with the opportunity, not the threat. Name the obvious reading, then give the better one: "The easy read is that clients will pay more. The better read is that you just got a new thing to sell."
  2. Facts precise enough to act on. Start date, region, who is affected, the price per unit, the cost in currency, who is not affected yet. If a reader cannot check it, cut it or source it.
  3. Name the offer and the price shape. "A fixed fee audit before their next renewal, then a monthly retainer" beats "a new recurring job". Show the invoice they could send.
  4. Find the part worth copying. The headline number is rarely the lesson. The non-obvious move behind it is.
  5. A concrete next action with a number. "A direct campaign to 50 manufacturers goes out before month end" beats a general principle.
  6. Name the usual move and its cost. "The usual move is to find a new lead source once the current one dries up. By then you are two quiet months behind."
  7. Land on a contrast pair. Two lines, same structure, opposite outcomes: "The platform sends leads when it suits the platform. Your own sources send leads when you build them."
  8. No question at the end of the body. If a question earns its place, it goes in the pinned comment as a simple either-or.
  9. Cut signposts. No "Here is what it means", no "The mechanics are simple". State the thing.
  10. No method labels in short-form, but always one decision theme shown in plain words.
  11. Short lines. One or two sentences per line, a break after each idea.
  12. Soft ask in the pinned comment, never in the body.

Closing lines. Short posts end on the contrast pair, no slogan. Longer pieces close with: "You have two options: do this yourself, or get some help. If you'd like some help, book here: [your booking link]"

CTA placement

  • Post body: none.
  • Pinned comment: a soft ask ("Want help to build your pipeline? Book a call on my profile.") or an either-or question.
  • Newsletter and campaigns: "Book here:" plus your link.
  • Links always in comments, never in the post body.

Setup and weekly routine

Setup takes about 4 hours, once. Daily execution is 20 minutes, the weekly review 45.

Before the first post

  • One vertical named in a single sentence: "I help [platform] partners serving [vertical] to [outcome]."
  • Profile banner, headline and About name that vertical. The profile is the destination, posts are the road signs.
  • At least three recorded client calls from the last 90 days to draw stories from.
  • A content log (sheet or Notion): date, post type, hook, result cited, saves, DMs.
  • One call-to-action destination, used in every pinned comment, DM and the profile Featured section.

Mine one recorded call a week

Pull three moments from the transcript: a win, a mistake, a pattern you named. Each becomes a post. Log it so no story repeats inside 90 days. Small wins and honest mistakes land better than big wins.

Routine

  • Daily (20 minutes): 10 drafting, 5 replying to comments on the last three posts, 5 sending three DMs to commenters or warm connections.
  • Weekly (45 minutes): pull last week's call and log three ideas, review what got saves, batch next week's three posts, cut what is not working.

90-day targets: 3 posts a week, 2 to 4 qualified DM conversations a week by week 6, 1 to 2 calls booked from LinkedIn a month by day 90. Behind at week 4? Sharpen the vertical first.

Proof, voice, hooks, measurement and roles

Proof standard

A named client outcome needs a recorded call transcript and a verified entry in a proof register before it is published. Market facts need a source a reader could check. Principles and patterns need no proof. Show results, never method. No client names without written permission. Until a recorded win exists for a claim, teach the pattern instead of inventing a case.

Voice standard

Short sentences. No em-dashes. Never open with "I" or the reader's name. A platform or industry named early. A person or a number in every post. Commercial language: rates, margin, retainer, fixed fee, pipeline, operating profit. Keep a banned-phrase list and enforce it.

Hooks

The first line opens a loop and does not answer the question it raises. Strong hook families: a vendor just changed the price of something, a client number that contradicts the obvious, the easy read against the better read, what your reader can now charge for.

Measurement

Tie content to one pipeline number, for example sales calls booked per week. Track leading indicators weekly: qualified DMs, newsletter replies, saves on market-signal and client posts. Run any change to the message as a four-week test against the previous four weeks. If nothing moves, fix the hook first and the audience second, never both at once.

Roles

One person produces drafts from this playbook. The owner edits and approves (red pen, black pen). One person owns the drafting tools and templates and updates them within 48 hours of any change to this document.
